Thought and its content
What the person believes and how the thinking is organised.
Delusion
a belief that is demonstrably untrue or not shared by others, usually based on incorrect inference about external reality.
Referential thinking
ideation, not held with delusional intensity, that random or coincidental events are of particular and unusual significance to the person.
Suspiciousness
ideation in which the behaviour of others is viewed with anxiety, mistrust or hostility, and perceived as potentially threatening.
Paranoid ideation
ideation, not held with delusional intensity, involving suspiciousness or beliefs of being harassed, persecuted or unfairly treated by others.
Disorganized thinking
a disturbance in the associative thought process, typically manifested in speech, in which the person shifts suddenly from one topic to another that is unrelated or minimally related to the first, and gives no indication of being aware of the disconnectedness or illogicality of their thinking.

Thought withdrawal
the experience that the person’s thoughts are being removed by an outside person or force.
Affect and mood
How feeling is experienced and how it shows.
Depressed mood
a negative affective state characterized by low mood, sadness, emptiness, hopelessness or dejection.
Elevated mood
a positive mood state typically characterized by increased energy and self-esteem, which may be out of proportion to the individual’s life circumstances.
Anhedonia
an inability to experience pleasure from normally pleasurable activities.
Irritability
a mood state characterized by being easily annoyed and provoked to anger, out of proportion to the circumstances.
Blunted affect
a severe reduction in the expressive range and intensity of affect, but less than is observed in Flat affect.
Flat affect
absence or near absence of any sign of affective expression.
Labile affect
marked variability in emotional expression, with repeated, rapid and abrupt shifts.
Constricted affect
a marked reduction in the expressive range and intensity of affect, but less than is observed in Blunted affect.
Inappropriate affect
affective expression that is discordant with the content of the person’s speech or ideation, or incompatible with the demands of a particular situation.
Apathy
a reduction or lack of feeling, emotion, interest or concern – a state of indifference.
Demoralization
loss of confidence in one’s ability to cope, with associated feelings of helplessness, hopelessness and discouragement.
Hopelessness
little or no belief in a positive future.
Drive and psychomotor activity
Motivation, movement and the pace of behaviour.
Avolition
a general lack of drive, or lack of motivation to pursue meaningful goals (e.g. as evidenced by limited participation in work, school or socializing with others).
Psychomotor agitation
excessive motor activity, usually manifested in purposeless behaviours such as fidgeting, shifting, fiddling, inability to sit or stand still, or wringing of the hands.
Psychomotor retardation
a visible generalized slowing of movements and speech.
Tiredness
a feeling of reduced alertness and an accompanying decrease in mental acuity, in some cases resulting in an impulse or tendency to fall asleep.
Social withdrawal
a retreat from relationships and other social interactions.
Mutism
a lack of verbal output that may be generalized or restricted to specific situations.
Anxiety and fear
The terms that separate fear, anxiety and worry from one another.
Fear
an emotional response to perceived imminent threat or danger associated with urges to flee or fight.
Anxiety
apprehensiveness or anticipation of future danger or misfortune accompanied by a feeling of worry, distress or somatic symptoms of tension.
Worry
unpleasant thoughts that are difficult to control, related to anticipated potential negative events.
Panic attack
a discrete episode of intense fear or apprehension accompanied by the rapid and concurrent onset of a number of characteristic symptoms.
Avoidance behaviour
the act of keeping away from circumstances, situations or stimuli that cause anxiety or other negative emotions in the individual.
Cognition
What the cognitive layer of each case is trying to measure.
Poor concentration
difficulty focusing attention and sustaining the mental energy necessary to accomplish a task or goal.
Distractibility
difficulty focusing on tasks, with attention being easily diverted by extraneous stimuli.
Impaired executive functioning
impairment in higher-level cognitive abilities, such as planning, sequencing, concept formation, abstracting and decision-making.
Perseveration
the persistent repetition of previously used words, phrases or details that are not responsive to the demands of the situation.
Impaired abstract thinking
an inability to use concepts and to make and understand generalizations, such as the identifying the properties or pattern shared by a variety of specific items or events.
Impaired judgement
a deficit in the capacity to make sound, reasoned and responsible decisions.
Risk
Terms that carry clinical weight and are easy to use loosely.
Suicidal ideation
thoughts, ideas or ruminations about the possibility of ending one’s life, ranging from thinking that one would be better off dead to formulation of elaborate plans.
Non-suicidal self-injury
intentional self-inflicted injury to the body – most commonly cutting, scraping, burning, biting or hitting – with the expectation that the injury will lead to only minor physical harm.
The parts of a CDDR diagnostic write-up
Every disorder in the CDDR manual is described using this same set of headings
Essential (Required) Features
The features a clinician should expect to see in every case of the disorder — the closest thing CDDR has to "criteria," though written as clinical description rather than a rigid checklist.
In this course — the "Diagnostic criteria reproduced verbatim from World Health Organization…" text on each case's ICD-11 step is the Essential Features section for that disorder.
Additional Clinical Features
Features commonly seen in some, but not all, cases of the disorder — useful for recognition, but not required for the diagnosis to apply.
Boundary with Normality (Threshold)
Where CDDR draws the line between an ordinary human experience (sadness, worry, unusual belief) and the disorder — the severity, duration, or impairment threshold that has to be crossed before a diagnosis is appropriate.
Differential Diagnosis (Boundaries with Other Disorders and Conditions)
How to tell this disorder apart from others it's commonly confused with, including other mental disorders and relevant medical conditions — the section that matters most when two diagnoses both seem to fit.
Course Features
How the disorder typically unfolds over time — onset pattern, typical duration, episodic vs. persistent course, and common trajectories.
Developmental Presentations
How the disorder can look different at different ages — for instance, presentations more typical in children or adolescents versus adults.
Culture-Related Features
How cultural context can shape how symptoms are expressed, interpreted, or reported, and what a clinician should account for across different cultural settings.
Sex- and/or Gender-Related Features
Known differences in presentation, prevalence, or expression of the disorder associated with sex or gender.
