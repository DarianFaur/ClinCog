Stop-signal task
Used in Jordan's case (substance use) – inhibitory control

On most trials, the respondent makes a fast choice response (e.g., left/right arrow key matching an arrow's direction). On a minority of trials (typically ~25%), a stop signal appears after a short, variable delay, instructing the respondent to withhold that response. The task is one of the most widely used laboratory measures of response inhibition across clinical, developmental, and comparative (animal) research.

Stop-Signal Reaction Time (SSRT)

SSRT is not observed directly – it is estimated from the horse-race model, which treats the go process and the stop process as independent runners: whichever finishes first determines whether the response is executed or withheld. By titrating the stop-signal delay (SSD) so that stopping succeeds on roughly half of stop trials, and combining SSD with the go-RT distribution, an estimate of the covert stopping latency (SSRT) can be derived. The 2019 consensus guide explicitly recommends the integration method over the older mean method for estimating SSRT, because the integration method is more robust to skew in the RT distribution.

The 2019 cross-lab consensus paper – 48 co-authors from the response-inhibition field – issued 12 concrete recommendations for running and analysing the stop-signal task (e.g., ~25% stop-signal rate, a staircase-tracking SSD procedure, the integration method for SSRT, minimum trial counts) specifically to stop avoidable methodological choices from silently biasing SSRT estimates and group comparisons.
SSRT deficits across disorders – and an honest caveat about substance use
  • Medium/large, well-replicated deficits
  • Substance use specifically: small, not statistically significant in the most recent meta-analysis

SSRT deficits (Hedges' g vs. healthy controls) are well-established and medium-to-large for OCD, schizophrenia, and ADHD. For substance use disorders specifically, the picture is less settled: the same 2010 meta-analysis that established those three deficits explicitly found SSRT to be "less impaired or normal" for substance dependence, and a more recent, publication-bias-checked 2024 re-analysis reached the same conclusion (Hedges' g = 0.13–0.27, not statistically significant) – even though an intervening 2014 meta-analysis (Smith et al.) had reported a deficit. This is a genuinely open, actively-revisited question in the field, not a settled fact in either direction. Sources: Lipszyc & Schachar (2010); Fascher et al. (2024).

Why it belongs in an addiction workup

Kwako et al.'s Addictions Neuroclinical Assessment (ANA) framework groups laboratory measures relevant to addictive disorders into three functional domains derived from the neurocircuitry of addiction – incentive salience, negative emotionality, and executive function – echoing Koob & Volkow's three-stage model of the addiction cycle (binge/intoxication, withdrawal/negative affect, preoccupation/anticipation). Response inhibition, measured with tasks such as the stop-signal task, sits within the executive function domain.
