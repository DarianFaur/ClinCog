Clinical Vignette

Read the case carefully before administering the assessment tools.

Darren, 34
High school English teacher · First psychiatric contact
Presentation

Darren is a 34-year-old high school English teacher. His general practitioner refers him after two weeks of sick leave. This is his first contact with mental health services. He arrives on time, neatly but carelessly dressed, and speaks slowly and quietly, often pausing before he answers. Asked how he is, he says: “What’s the point? Nothing I do matters.”

History

Darren was a well-liked teacher, known for lively lessons and a quick sense of humour. About two months ago, after a difficult term, he began to feel flat and tired for most of the day, nearly every day, and over the following weeks this deepened. He missed several grading deadlines and, three weeks ago, walked out of a lesson halfway through, close to tears. Since then he has been calling in sick. He has never had a period of unusually high mood, reduced need for sleep or overactivity, and describes himself as “always the steady one”.

Current difficulties

Most mornings Darren lies in bed for hours after the alarm, feeling numb. Books and running, which he used to love, “feel like nothing now”. He falls asleep easily but wakes around four and cannot get back to sleep, going over his failures, real and imagined. He has little appetite, often skips meals, and his clothes have become loose. He says his mind is “in fog”: planning a simple meal or an email to his head of department feels impossible, and he cannot decide what to do first. He feels he has let his students and colleagues down and calls himself “a fraud”. He sees no future in which things improve. Small things make him irritable, which surprises him, and at other times he feels nothing at all. Colleagues have noticed that he moves and speaks more slowly than before.

Daily life and relationships

Darren lives alone. He has stopped replying to colleagues’ messages and has cancelled his weekly call with his sister, telling people he is busy with marking. He spends most days on the couch with the television on, not really watching it. He still showers and dresses every morning, “because otherwise I’d have nothing left”. Shopping, bills and household tasks are piling up.

Background

Darren has no previous psychiatric history and no significant medical illness; recent blood tests, including thyroid function, are normal. He drinks one or two glasses of wine at weekends and does not use drugs. His mother was treated for depression when he was a teenager. Over the past month he has increasingly thought that everyone would be better off if he were gone. Last week, for the first time, he thought about how he might end his life; the thought frightened him, and he has told no one. On one occasion he pressed his fingernails into his forearm until it hurt, saying only that it made “the noise inside” quieter for a moment. He accepts an urgent follow-up appointment and agrees to a safety plan.

A depressive episode requires the concurrent presence of at least five of the ten characteristic symptoms below, occurring most of the day, nearly every day, during a period lasting at least 2 weeks. At least one symptom from the affective cluster must be present. Assessment of the presence or absence of symptoms should be made relative to the typical functioning of the individual. Depressive Episode — Core and Associated Symptoms
Affective cluster
a)
Depressed mood as reported by the individual (e.g. feeling down, sad) or as observed (e.g. tearful, defeated appearance) (Note: in children and adolescents depressed mood can manifest as irritability.)
b)
Markedly diminished interest or pleasure in activities, especially those normally found to be enjoyable to the individual (Note: this may include a reduction in sexual desire.)
