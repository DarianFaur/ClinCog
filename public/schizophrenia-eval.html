Clinical Vignette

Dennis, 25

College student · First psychiatric contact

Dennis, a 25-year-old college student, had always been known for his academic excellence and enthusiasm for learning. Over the past year, his friends and family began noticing subtle but concerning changes. He became increasingly withdrawn, spending long hours alone in his room, barely engaging in conversations, and showing little interest in activities he once enjoyed. His once vibrant personality dulled, and his academic performance declined significantly — his professors noted he struggled to concentrate, and his work became disorganized, often incomplete or lacking coherence when submitted at all. His hygiene deteriorated, with unwashed clothes and an unkempt appearance becoming the norm. His family initially attributed these changes to academic stress.

In recent weeks, Dennis’s condition escalated dramatically. One evening, his parents found him pacing frantically around the house, muttering to himself and casting nervous glances at the windows. When asked what was wrong, he responded cryptically, insisting that government agents had planted cameras in their home to monitor him. He became increasingly agitated, accusing his family of conspiring against him, and refused to eat food prepared by his mother, convinced it was poisoned. At times he would burst into laughter for no apparent reason or engage in heated arguments with unseen entities, spending hours responding to voices no one else could hear.

His speech became erratic, often shifting from one topic to another without any logical connection — he would start a sentence but lose his train of thought midway, jumping to unrelated subjects. His family grew deeply concerned when he began wandering the streets at night, dressed in mismatched clothes, gesturing wildly at invisible figures. On one occasion, he was found standing motionless in the backyard, staring at the sky, whispering about secret messages hidden in the stars.

Dennis’s deteriorating condition reached a critical point when he attempted to barricade himself in his room, convinced that shadowy figures were coming to take him away. This is his first psychiatric contact.

Differential Diagnosis of Psychotic Disorders
1) Schizophrenia Criteria (C1–C6)

Based on everything documented above, does the case meet each DSM-5 criterion for Schizophrenia?

C1
Criterion A — Two or more characteristic symptoms (delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, grossly disorganized/catatonic behavior, negative symptoms) present for a significant portion of a 1-month period, with at least one being delusions, hallucinations, or disorganized speech.
C2
Criterion B — Level of functioning in work, relationships, or self-care is markedly below the level achieved before onset.
C3
Criterion C — Continuous signs of disturbance persist for at least 6 months, including at least 1 month of active-phase symptoms (or less if successfully treated).
C4
Criterion D — Schizoaffective disorder and depressive or bipolar disorder with psychotic features have been ruled out (no major mood episode concurrent with active-phase symptoms, or mood episodes brief relative to psychotic duration).
C5
Criterion E — The disturbance is not attributable to the physiological effects of a substance or another medical condition.
C6
Criterion F — If there is a history of autism spectrum disorder or a childhood communication disorder, this is either not applicable, or prominent delusions/hallucinations have also been present for at least 1 month.
