Clinical Vignette

Read the case carefully before administering the assessment tools.

Dennis, 25
College student · First psychiatric contact
Presentation

Dennis, a 25-year-old college student, had always been known for his academic excellence and enthusiasm for learning. Over the past year, his friends and family began noticing subtle but concerning changes. He became increasingly withdrawn, spending long hours alone in his room, barely engaging in conversations, and showing little interest in activities he once enjoyed. His once vibrant personality dulled, and his academic performance declined significantly — his professors noted he struggled to concentrate, and his work became disorganized, often incomplete or lacking coherence when submitted at all. His hygiene deteriorated, with unwashed clothes and an unkempt appearance becoming the norm. His family initially attributed these changes to academic stress.

Acute Psychotic Symptoms

In recent weeks, Dennis’s condition escalated dramatically. One evening, his parents found him pacing frantically around the house, muttering to himself and casting nervous glances at the windows. When asked what was wrong, he responded cryptically, insisting that government agents had planted cameras in their home to monitor him. At times he would burst into laughter for no apparent reason or engage in heated arguments with unseen entities, spending hours responding to voices no one else could hear.

He became increasingly agitated, accusing his family of conspiring against him, and refused to eat food prepared by his mother, convinced it was poisoned.
Behavioral & Speech Changes

His speech became erratic, often shifting from one topic to another without any logical connection — he would start a sentence but lose his train of thought midway, jumping to unrelated subjects. His family grew deeply concerned when he began wandering the streets at night, dressed in mismatched clothes, gesturing wildly at invisible figures. On one occasion, he was found standing motionless in the backyard, staring at the sky, whispering about secret messages hidden in the stars.

Presenting Crisis

Dennis’s deteriorating condition reached a critical point when he attempted to barricade himself in his room, convinced that shadowy figures were coming to take him away. This is his first psychiatric contact.

Layer 1 · Categorical · ICD-11

Let's confirm this by rating the clinical symptoms described by the character through structured questions.

Rate each symptom domain from the ICD-11 Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements (CDDR) for Schizophrenia (6A20), reproduced verbatim below.

Essential (Required) Features

At least two of the following symptoms must be present (by the individual’s report or through observation by the clinician or other informants) most of the time for a period of 1 month or more. At least one of the qualifying symptoms should be from items a) to d) below:

a)
Persistent delusions (e.g., grandiose delusions, delusions of reference, persecutory delusions).
↳ One of the two most robust indicators of psychotic disorder, especially when bizarre or mood-incongruent.
b)
Persistent hallucinations (most commonly auditory, although they may be in any sensory modality).
↳ Auditory hallucinations are the most common presentation in schizophrenia; note modality and content when documenting.
c)
Disorganized thinking (formal thought disorder) (e.g., tangentiality and loose associations, irrelevant speech, neologisms) – when severe, the person’s speech may be so incoherent as to be incomprehensible (“word salad”);
↳ A disturbance in the form of thought, not just its content — often directly observable in speech during interview.
d)
Experiences of influence, passivity or control (i.e., the experience that one's feelings, impulses, actions or thoughts are not generated by oneself, are being placed in one's mind or withdrawn from one's mind by others, or that one's thoughts are being broadcast to others).
↳ Historically considered a first-rank symptom highly specific to schizophrenia, though ICD-11 no longer weights it above other domains.
e)
Negative symptoms such as affective flattening, alogia or paucity of speech, avolition, asociality and anhedonia.
↳ Often the most functionally impairing domain over time, yet easily under-recognized against more visible positive symptoms.
f)
Grossly disorganized behaviour that impedes goal-directed activity (e.g., behaviour that appears bizarre or purposeless, unpredictable or inappropriate emotional responses that interferes with the ability to organize behaviour).
↳ Distinct from the exclusion criterion below — this refers to behavioural disorganization itself, not a medical or substance cause.
g)
Psychomotor disturbances such as catatonic restlessness or agitation, posturing, waxy flexibility, negativism, mutism, or stupor.
↳ Includes catatonic presentations; ICD-11 folds these directly into the core symptom list rather than treating them as a separate specifier.
Exclusion Criterion
✕
The symptoms are not a manifestation of another medical condition (e.g., a brain tumour) and are not due to the effects of a substance or medication (e.g., corticosteroids) on the central nervous system, including withdrawal effects (e.g., from alcohol).

Letter–Number Span — hold and mentally reorder increasingly long strings of letters and numbers; taps working memory capacity, one of the most consistently impaired domains in schizophrenia.
