Clinical staging
Where a person sits along the course of an illness, rather than which category they fall into

Clinical staging borrows from oncology and cardiology: the same diagnosis at an early stage and a late stage is not the same clinical problem, and should not attract the same treatment. Applied to mental health, it asks where someone is along a continuum of illness – at risk, first symptoms, first episode, recurrence, treatment resistance – and uses that position to choose the least intensive intervention likely to work, and to estimate prognosis.

Is there a standard body, like HiTOP or NIMH? No. Staging has no consortium, no official model and no canonical matrix. Several models have been proposed for schizophrenia alone – among them Fava and Kellner's, Lieberman's, Singh's, Agius's, Cosci's and Fountoulakis's – and reviews note that McGorry's model “may be one of the most developed classifications” with sub-stages that adapt to outpatient and inpatient settings (Hamieh et al., 2023). The staging work with the widest reach comes from Orygen and the Centre for Youth Mental Health in Melbourne; the stage definitions below are the ones used in that literature.
The stages
StageDefinition
0Asymptomatic but at risk.
1aHelp-seeking with distress: mild or non-specific symptoms.
1bAttenuated syndromes: moderate but sub-threshold symptoms, with some diagnostic specificity.
2Full-threshold disorder – a first treated episode.
3Recurrence or persistence.
4Treatment resistance: unremitting illness in someone who has received mental health services for at least two years.

Stage definitions as summarised in Eaton et al. (2023), Nature Reviews Psychology.

Staged care is not stepped care

The distinction matters clinically, and the review draws it sharply: stepped care “responds belatedly to a relapse or worsening of a condition, whereas staged care – like cancer treatment – aims to pre-empt onset, progression and relapse” (Eaton et al., 2023).

These are ranges across cohorts, not norms for an individual. A stage is a clinical judgement about a course, and the same caution applies as to every other measure in this application: one assessment, at one moment, does not establish a trajectory.
Three papers to start from
  • Where staging sits among the alternatives. Eaton, N. R., Bringmann, L. F., Elmer, T., Fried, E. I., Forbes, M. K., Greene, A. L., Krueger, R. F., Kotov, R., McGorry, P. D., Mei, C., & Waszczuk, M. A. (2023). A review of approaches and models in psychopathology conceptualization research. Nature Reviews Psychology, 2, 622–636. https://doi.org/10.1038/s44159-023-00218-4
    Reviews transdiagnostic dimensional models, network approaches and clinical staging side by side – the single most useful entry point, and the source of the stage definitions and figures above.
  • Can stages be derived from data rather than consensus? Ratheesh, A., Hammond, D., Gao, C., Marwaha, S., Thompson, A., Hartmann, J., Davey, C., Zammit, S., Berk, M., McGorry, P., & Nelson, B. (2023). Empirically driven transdiagnostic stages in the development of mood, anxiety and psychotic symptoms in a cohort of youth followed from birth. Translational Psychiatry, 13, 103. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-023-02396-4
    Uses the ALSPAC birth cohort – 3,269 young people with complete symptom-stage data, 64.3% female – and finds that Stage 1b depressive, anxiety and psychotic stages are inter-related and share risk factors (female sex, emotional and behavioural difficulties in early adolescence, life events in late adolescence), while hypomania is not related to any of them. The authors conclude anxiety, psychotic and depressive symptoms could be combined into a single transdiagnostic stage in this cohort; hypomania could not.
  • Does stage track clinical severity? Hamieh, F., Hallit, S., Haddad, C., Obeid, S., & Kazour, F. (2023). Correlates of severity in a clinical staging model of schizophrenia: a cross-sectional study among 158 subjects. BMC Psychiatry, 23, 648. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-023-05144-6
    158 inpatients, staged with the McGorry model: 43% at relapse (3b), 47.5% multiple relapses (3c), 9.5% persistent and severe illness (Stage 4). Stage 4 patients had longer hospitalisations, higher PANSS totals and subscales, higher chlorpromazine-equivalent doses and more antipsychotic trials; Stage 3b patients had the highest mean GAF. Cross-sectional, single site, inpatients only – so it shows that stages separate on severity, not that staging predicts anything.
