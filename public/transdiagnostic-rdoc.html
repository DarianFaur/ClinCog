Research Domain Criteria (RDoC)
A research framework from the US National Institute of Mental Health

RDoC starts from the opposite end to a diagnostic manual. Rather than beginning with a disorder and looking for its mechanism, it begins with a dimension of normal functioning – threat response, reward learning, working memory – and studies how it varies from healthy to impaired, across whoever happens to show that variation. NIMH is unambiguous about its scope:

“RDoC is not meant to serve as a diagnostic guide, nor is it intended to replace current diagnostic systems.” National Institute of Mental Health, About RDoC

That is worth holding on to when reading RDoC-framed studies: a finding that a circuit differs between groups is a finding about that circuit, not a new diagnosis.

The matrix

The framework is organised as a grid. Rows are constructs, grouped into six domains of functioning; columns are units of analysis, running from genes up to the whole person. A study picks a construct and measures it across one or more units.

Foundations of RDoC

Insel and colleagues set out three assumptions underlying RDoC: that mental illnesses are “disorders of brain circuits”; that this circuit dysfunction can be identified with the tools of clinical neuroscience, “including electrophysiology, functional neuroimaging, and new methods for quantifying connections in vivo”; and that genetics and clinical neuroscience will yield “biosignatures that will augment clinical symptoms and signs for clinical management” (Insel et al., 2010). The matrix itself is described there as rows of constructs grouped into broad domains of function, with columns denoting “different levels of analysis, from genetic, molecular, and cellular levels, proceeding to the circuit-level … and on to the level of the individual, family environment, and social context.”

Note: the founding paper is candid about how far this was from practice. “Clearly, this is a vision for the future, given the rudimentary nature of data relating measures of brain function to clinically relevant individual differences.” The authors add that RDoC “will likely require several mid-course corrections and may ultimately fail to deliver the transformation we seek in clinical care” (Insel et al., 2010). Fifteen years on, that framing is still the honest one to teach: RDoC has reorganised a great deal of research without replacing a single diagnosis.
