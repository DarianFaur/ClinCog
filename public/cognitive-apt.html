Alcohol Purchase Task (behavioural economics)
Used in Jordan's case (substance use) – reinforcing value / demand for alcohol

A hypothetical purchase task asks how many standard drinks the respondent would buy and consume at each of a series of escalating prices (from free up to a price high enough that consumption drops to zero). Plotting consumption against price produces an individual demand curve, adapted for alcohol specifically by Murphy & MacKillop (2006) from the general hypothetical-purchase-task framework of Jacobs & Bickel (1999).

The demand-curve indices
IndexWhat it captures
Intensity (Q0)Consumption when price = 0 – demand at no constraint
BreakpointFirst price at which consumption drops to zero
OmaxMaximum expenditure reached, across all price points
PmaxThe price at which Omax occurs – where demand shifts from inelastic to elastic
Elasticity (α)Derived rate at which consumption falls as price rises, from the exponentiated demand equation

Higher Q0, higher Omax, and a later (higher-price) breakpoint or Pmax all indicate a stronger reinforcing value of alcohol for that individual – i.e., alcohol is harder to "price out" of their behaviour.

In the original validation (267 college student drinkers), demand-curve indices derived from the Alcohol Purchase Task were well described by an exponential demand equation and correlated with independent measures of drinking quantity and alcohol-related problems, supporting the task as a brief, non-invasive proxy for a costly laboratory self-administration paradigm (Murphy & MacKillop, 2006).
