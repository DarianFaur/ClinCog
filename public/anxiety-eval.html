Seminar Activity

Three Ways to Evaluate
Social Anxiety Disorder

A multi-level clinical and cognitive assessment exercise

This activity guides you through three distinct frameworks for evaluating Social Anxiety Disorder — from categorical diagnosis to dimensional psychometrics to implicit cognitive processing. Each layer illuminates a different facet of the disorder that the others cannot fully capture.

  • Layer 1
    Categorical Evaluation
    ICD-11 criteria assessed via structured interview, reproduced verbatim from the CDDR — a checklist yielding a binary diagnostic decision.
  • Layer 2
    Dimensional–Psychological Evaluation
    GAD-7 for severity quantification, plus selected HiTOP subfactors (Fear, Distress, Disinhibited Externalizing) for transdiagnostic dimensionality.
  • Layer 3
    Dimensional–Cognitive Evaluation
    Dot-probe attentional bias task measuring implicit threat-related vigilance — the neuropsychological substrate of anxious apprehension that self-report measures cannot access.

You will encounter a patient vignette first, then complete each evaluation layer in sequence. A comparative report is auto-generated at the end. All sections must be completed before proceeding.

Clinical Vignette

Read the case carefully before administering the assessment tools.

Alex, 24
Undergraduate student · Referred by general practitioner
Presentation

Alex is a 24-year-old undergraduate student referred by their general practitioner because of long-standing difficulties with studying and with other people. In the first session Alex sits near the door, keeps their coat on and speaks quietly, glancing up only briefly, and asks at the start whether the session is being recorded. “I know it sounds silly,” Alex says, “but I just feel like everyone is watching me.”

History

Alex remembers feeling this way since early adolescence, when reading aloud in class made them feel sick for days beforehand. The fear has been present without a break for at least three years and became much worse after Alex started university. Alex has always explained it as shyness and has never asked for help before. A school counsellor once described Alex as “just quiet”, and Alex accepted that.

Current difficulties

Alex feels intense fear in situations where others might observe or judge them: speaking in seminars, eating in the canteen, making phone calls or meeting new people. In seminars Alex rarely speaks, convinced that anything they say will sound foolish. When called on unexpectedly, their heart races, their face turns red and their throat feels tight. Afterwards Alex goes over the conversation for hours, picking apart their tone, words and expression. Alex watches other people’s faces closely for signs of boredom or disapproval. The physical symptoms happen only in social situations: Alex has never had a sudden attack of fear out of the blue, and is comfortable in crowds, lifts and public transport as long as no one expects them to talk. Alex worries about exams and money, as most students do, but says these worries feel manageable. Alex recognises that the fear is out of proportion: “People probably aren’t paying that much attention to me, but knowing that doesn’t help.”

Daily life and relationships

Alex has turned down two part-time job offers in the past year because both involved working with customers. Last month Alex left a university networking event after forty minutes, shaking and close to tears, and went home without speaking to anyone. Alex has cancelled plans with friends several times, blaming headaches, and their grades have dropped because seminar participation counts towards assessment. Alex has two close friends from school and feels at ease with them, and lives with a flatmate but avoids the shared kitchen when others are there.

Background

Alex has no significant medical history and takes no medication. They drink rarely, at most one drink at a party, and do not use drugs. There is no history of unusual perceptual experiences. Alex describes their mood as “a bit low” and links this directly to feeling left out; sleep and appetite are normal, and they still enjoy reading and gaming alone. Their mother is described as “quite anxious too”. During the interview Alex is tense and makes little eye contact; their speech is quiet but coherent, and they understand that the fear is excessive.

Layer 1 — Categorical Evaluation

ICD-11 / CDDR

Social Anxiety Disorder (6B04)

Rate each item as present (+), absent (−), or uncertain (?) based on the vignette and your clinical judgement. All items must be rated before proceeding.

a)

Marked and excessive fear or anxiety that occurs consistently in one or more social situations such as social interactions (e.g. having a conversation), doing something while feeling observed (e.g. eating or drinking in the presence of others) or performing in front of others (e.g. giving a speech) is required for diagnosis.

↳ The core feature. Note the CDDR wording is “occurs consistently”, not “consistently occurs”, and that the examples are part of the criterion.
b)

The individual is concerned that they will act in a way, or show anxiety symptoms, that will be negatively evaluated by others (i.e. be humiliating, be embarrassing, lead to rejection or be offensive).

↳ This is what distinguishes social anxiety from generalized anxiety — the fear is specifically about others’ evaluation.
c)

Relevant social situations are consistently avoided or endured with intense fear or anxiety.

↳ Either active avoidance or enduring the situation with marked distress satisfies this criterion.
d)

The symptoms are not transient – that is, they persist for an extended period of time (e.g. at least several months).

↳ The CDDR states a duration in terms of persistence rather than a fixed cut-off.
e)

The symptoms are not better accounted for by another mental disorder (e.g. agoraphobia, body dysmorphic disorder, olfactory reference disorder).

↳ Differential diagnosis, stated as a required feature in the CDDR. This criterion was missing from the earlier version of this page.
f)

The symptoms result in significant distress about experiencing persistent anxiety symptoms or significant impairment in personal, family, social, educational, occupational or other important areas of functioning. If functioning is maintained, it is only through significant additional effort.

↳ Distress about the anxiety itself counts, as does impairment. Maintained functioning achieved only through significant extra effort still meets this criterion.
