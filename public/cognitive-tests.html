MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB)
Used in Dennis's case (psychosis) — Speed of Processing and Working Memory domains

The MCCB was developed by the NIMH-funded Measurement and Treatment Research to Improve Cognition in Schizophrenia (MATRICS) initiative to give clinical trials a standard, psychometrically vetted way to measure cognitive change in schizophrenia. A committee evaluated more than 90 candidate tests across seven cognitive domains, narrowed these to a 20-test beta battery, and then selected the final 10 tests based on test–retest reliability, practicality, and tolerability data from 176 people with schizophrenia.

Scoring

Each test's raw score is converted to an age- and (where applicable) gender-corrected T-score (M = 50, SD = 10) against a community normative sample, then combined into the seven domain scores and an overall composite. There is no fixed minimum/maximum — T-scores are continuous.

What the two tests used in this case measure

Trail Making Test, Part A — connect numbered circles in ascending order as fast as possible; a relatively pure visuomotor processing-speed measure, one of the domains most consistently and severely impaired in first-episode and chronic psychosis. Letter–Number Span — an examiner reads a mixed string of letters and numbers aloud; the respondent must reorder and repeat back numbers first (ascending) then letters (alphabetically), taxing working-memory storage and manipulation together.

Speed of Processing is consistently reported as one of the most impaired MCCB domains in schizophrenia, with effect sizes in the large range in meta-analytic comparisons against healthy controls — larger, on average, than most other MCCB domains.

CANTAB — Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery
Used in Darren's case (depression) — Spatial Working Memory and One Touch Stockings

CANTAB is a computerised, touchscreen neuropsychological battery developed at the University of Cambridge and now maintained commercially by Cambridge Cognition. Its tasks are largely language- and culture-independent, which has made it a common choice across neurology, psychiatry, and pharmacology trials — dementia, schizophrenia, depression, Parkinson's disease, and others.

Spatial Working Memory (SWM)

A number of coloured boxes are shown on screen. The participant searches them one at a time to find a hidden yellow token, using the rule that once a token has been found in a box, that box will not hide a token again for the rest of that trial — so returning to a box already emptied is an error. Box count increases across trials (up to 12). The key outcome, between errors, counts returns to boxes already shown to be empty; a separate strategy score captures how systematically the participant searches (e.g., always starting from the same box). It is described by Cambridge Cognition as sensitive to frontal-lobe / executive dysfunction.

One Touch Stockings of Cambridge (OTS)

Based on the Tower of London / Tower of Hanoi paradigm. Two displays of three coloured balls, shown as if held in stockings hanging from a beam, are presented; the participant must work out — without moving anything — the minimum number of moves needed to turn the lower display into the upper one, then touch the number box showing that answer. It measures spatial planning and working memory together, and is treated as a marker of frontal/executive function.

In a study using an automated neuropsychological battery (CANTAB) with 30 patients with major depressive disorder (10 currently symptomatic, 10 remitted, 10 healthy controls), the symptomatic group showed significant dysfunction on almost every test administered except IED (set-shifting) and MTS (matching to sample); visual memory was the most impaired domain. The remitted group still showed significant dysfunction except on IED, RTI (reaction time), and SOC (Stockings of Cambridge) — consistent with the broader finding that some CANTAB-measured deficits in depression persist beyond the symptomatic episode (Egerházi et al., 2013).
The evidence base for SWM deficits in depression

Bar length here represents the size of the pooled evidence base (number of participants across all included studies), not a score comparison — the meta-analysis reports this deficit as a standardized effect size (SMD) rather than a single raw score, since it pools many different studies. Dot-probe task
Used in Alex's case (social anxiety) — attentional bias to threat

The dot-probe paradigm was introduced by MacLeod, Mathews, & Tata (1986) to measure where attention is drawn without relying on self-report. Two stimuli — words or faces, one neutral and one emotionally salient (e.g., threatening) — appear briefly side by side on screen, then both disappear and a small probe (a dot, or in later variants a letter) appears in the location where one of them had been. The respondent presses a button to indicate the probe's location as fast as possible.

Logic of the measure

If attention was already drawn toward the emotional stimulus at the moment it disappeared, probes replacing it are detected faster than probes replacing the neutral stimulus. The attentional bias index is typically computed as the difference in reaction time between trials where the probe appears in the neutral-stimulus location versus the threat-stimulus location; a positive value indicates vigilance toward threat, a negative value indicates avoidance of it.

A systematic review of facial dot-probe studies in social anxiety found that socially anxious individuals reliably show heightened attention toward negative/angry faces compared with neutral faces, but no consistent bias for positive faces — i.e., the effect is specific to threat-relevant material, not a general attentional difference (Bantin et al., 2016).
Attentional bias to threat: anxious vs. non-anxious groups

Distributions are illustrative (shifted by the reported effect size, not raw score data) — they show what a medium, Cohen's d = 0.45 group difference actually looks like: real and reliable in aggregate across 172 studies, but with substantial overlap between individuals in each group. Bias toward threat is consistently higher in anxious samples, not an all-or-nothing marker. Source: Bar-Haim et al. (2007), meta-analysis of 172 studies, N=2,263 anxious vs. N=1,768 non-anxious.

A note on reliability

Traditional reaction-time dot-probe indices have repeatedly been reported to have poor internal consistency and test–retest reliability across the wider attentional-bias literature — a methodological concern worth raising with students alongside the task itself, not a reason to discard the paradigm.

Stop-signal task
Used in Jordan's case (substance use) — inhibitory control

On most trials, the respondent makes a fast choice response (e.g., left/right arrow key matching an arrow's direction). On a minority of trials (typically ~25%), a stop signal appears after a short, variable delay, instructing the respondent to withhold that response. The task is one of the most widely used laboratory measures of response inhibition across clinical, developmental, and comparative (animal) research.

Stop-Signal Reaction Time (SSRT)

SSRT is not observed directly — it is estimated from the horse-race model, which treats the go process and the stop process as independent runners: whichever finishes first determines whether the response is executed or withheld. By titrating the stop-signal delay (SSD) so that stopping succeeds on roughly half of stop trials, and combining SSD with the go-RT distribution, an estimate of the covert stopping latency (SSRT) can be derived. The 2019 consensus guide explicitly recommends the integration method over the older mean method for estimating SSRT, because the integration method is more robust to skew in the RT distribution.

The 2019 cross-lab consensus paper — 48 co-authors from the response-inhibition field — issued 12 concrete recommendations for running and analysing the stop-signal task (e.g., ~25% stop-signal rate, a staircase-tracking SSD procedure, the integration method for SSRT, minimum trial counts) specifically to stop avoidable methodological choices from silently biasing SSRT estimates and group comparisons.
SSRT deficits across disorders — and an honest caveat about substance use

SSRT deficits (Hedges' g vs. healthy controls) are well-established and medium-to-large for OCD, schizophrenia, and ADHD. For substance use disorders specifically, the picture is less settled: the same 2010 meta-analysis that established those three deficits explicitly found SSRT to be "less impaired or normal" for substance dependence, and a more recent, publication-bias-checked 2023 re-analysis reached the same conclusion (Hedges' g = 0.13–0.27, not statistically significant) — even though an intervening 2014 meta-analysis (Smith et al.) had reported a deficit. This is a genuinely open, actively-revisited question in the field, not a settled fact in either direction. Sources: Lipszyc & Schachar (2010); Fascher et al. (2023).

Why it belongs in an addiction workup

Kwako et al.'s Addictions Neuroclinical Assessment (ANA) framework groups laboratory measures relevant to addictive disorders into three functional domains derived from the neurocircuitry of addiction — incentive salience, negative emotionality, and executive function — echoing Koob & Volkow's three-stage model of the addiction cycle (binge/intoxication, withdrawal/negative affect, preoccupation/anticipation). Response inhibition, measured with tasks such as the stop-signal task, sits within the executive function domain.

Alcohol Purchase Task (behavioural economics)
Used in Jordan's case (substance use) — reinforcing value / demand for alcohol

A hypothetical purchase task asks how many standard drinks the respondent would buy and consume at each of a series of escalating prices (from free up to a price high enough that consumption drops to zero). Plotting consumption against price produces an individual demand curve, adapted for alcohol specifically by Murphy & MacKillop (2006) from the general hypothetical-purchase-task framework of Jacobs & Bickel (1999).

The demand-curve indices
IndexWhat it captures
Intensity (Q0)Consumption when price = 0 — demand at no constraint
BreakpointFirst price at which consumption drops to zero
OmaxMaximum expenditure reached, across all price points
PmaxThe price at which Omax occurs — where demand shifts from inelastic to elastic
Elasticity (α)Derived rate at which consumption falls as price rises, from the exponentiated demand equation

Higher Q0, higher Omax, and a later (higher-price) breakpoint or Pmax all indicate a stronger reinforcing value of alcohol for that individual — i.e., alcohol is harder to "price out" of their behaviour.

In the original validation (267 college student drinkers), demand-curve indices derived from the Alcohol Purchase Task were well described by an exponential demand equation and correlated with independent measures of drinking quantity and alcohol-related problems, supporting the task as a brief, non-invasive proxy for a costly laboratory self-administration paradigm (Murphy & MacKillop, 2006).
